# Divus Factus — Game Design Overview

> In the beginning…
> Man created God.

The player is not an ancient deity. The player is an **egregore**: a being called into
existence by collective human belief, sustained by it, and — critically — *defined* by it.

---

## The central inversion

Most god games treat belief as mana plus experience points: villagers pray, a bar fills,
the player spends it from a menu of powers they chose. Divus Factus inverts that.

**You can only do what they believe you can do.**

If nobody in the settlement has ever connected you to water, you cannot make rain. Not
"rain is locked pending 500 faith" — the concept does not exist. The way you gain rain is
to be present at rain, repeatedly and legibly, until a storyteller draws the link and it
enters doctrine.

This does three things no faith-as-currency system does:

1. **Every miracle becomes an act of communication** with an unreliable receiver, not a
   transaction.
2. **The tech tree is authored by NPCs.** The player influences it; the player does not
   write it.
3. **Failure becomes interesting.** You end a plague; they conclude you demanded the
   sacrifice that preceded it. Now you are the god who wants blood, doctrine has inertia,
   and merciful acts cost more because they contradict what is already known about you.

The gap between *what you did* and *what they think you did* is the game.

## The rhythm: peacetime teaches, crisis spends

Because power is granted by doctrine, the drought is not the moment you solve the problem —
it is the exam on work you did three seasons earlier. Calm stretches become load-bearing
instead of dead time waiting for a bar to refill, and a run can be lost long before it
looks lost.

## Steering without dictating

The player must be able to nudge interpretation. None of these levers is "select the
doctrine you want"; all of them cost the scarcest resource, which is attention.

1. **Association through repetition.** Be present when it rains. Slow, cheap, reliable.
2. **Timing.** The same act reads differently by moment. Snatching a villager mid-hunt
   is protection; snatching one at a funeral is wrath.
3. **Staging the audience.** You cannot edit doctrine, but you can move the *interpreters*.
   Carry the storyteller to where the flood is about to hit. This is why the Divine Hand
   is the primary input device and not a physics toy: it is the belief system's main
   instrument.
4. **Signatures.** Always precede rain with a breeze and eventually the breeze alone means
   *the god is coming*. A shared vocabulary, built over time, where cheap signs do the work
   of expensive miracles.
5. **Prophets.** Mark an individual and their reading of you is weighted enormously.
   Strongest lever, highest risk — prophets have their own personalities and ambitions.
6. **Silence.** Answer one faction's prayers and not another's. Free, slow, and
   thematically exact.

## Two rules that protect the premise

**Doctrine is never shown as a stat block.** The player sees what villagers *say* — quoted,
in their own words, through prayer and gossip. The numbers exist and are visible in debug
mode, but in play the belief system is delivered as people talking about you.

This is also the answer to "how does the player perceive simulation depth?" Dwarf
Fortress's depth is legible because it is text. Pixel figures at camera distance are dots.
**Prayer and rumour are the UI for the entire simulation.**

**Misinterpretation is never random.** It is motivated — derived from personality, from
what else happened nearby, from doctrine already held. Random misreading feels like the
game cheating. Motivated misreading feels like people.

## Resources

- **Belief** — the size and conviction of the following. Not spent. It is the foundation of
  the player's existence: it sets the ceiling on power, range of influence, and which
  concepts are available at all.
- **Divine Power** — the spendable resource, generated by active believers, prayer, sacred
  sites and ritual.
- **Coherence** (planned) — an egregore is sustained by a *shared* idea. When doctrine
  fragments into contradictory schisms, the player weakens. Losing coherence is how you
  die. This is what makes religious politics mechanical rather than decorative.

## Visual direction

HD-2D in look, not in technique. The signature of that style is its post-processing and
lighting discipline — tilt-shift depth of field, heavy bloom, hard warm/cool separation,
pixel-art surfaces on 3D geometry — none of which requires billboard sprites. Sprites
would force a locked camera, and this is a god game that needs to orbit.

So: **procedurally generated voxel creatures under HD-2D lighting, rendered at low
resolution and snapped to a fixed palette.** Tilt-shift makes real scenes look like
miniature models on a table, which for a game about a being looking down at tiny people it
invented is not a borrowed style — it is the fantasy rendered literally.

Every visual is generated from code. There are no binary art assets, no sprite sheets, no
imported rigs. Bodies are ~20 numbers, which makes them **heritable**: children are
generated by mixing their parents' genomes, so family resemblance is visible from the
god's-eye view before the simulation says anything about who is related to whom.

The payoff this unlocks: because appearance is data and doctrine is data, **you can see the
religion**. Villagers will wear their beliefs, and shrine geometry will be generated from
doctrine tags rather than picked from a prefab list — so a schism produces two visibly
different shrines in the same valley. Hand-authored art cannot do that at this scale.

## What is deliberately *not* in the first slice

Save/load, event-retention tiers, households and family trees, weather beyond rain, most of
the ability tree, and any procedural theology. The vertical slice exists to answer one
question — *is touching this world enjoyable?* — because no amount of simulation depth
rescues a god game whose hand feels bad.
